The Digital Multimeter (DMM)

The Keysight 34461A is the most precise instrument on your bench. This lab teaches its four jobs (DC volts, DC current, resistance, continuity), uses it to check Kirchhoff’s voltage law, and shows two traps every engineer falls into once: plugging into the wrong input, and the meter changing the circuit it measures.

2 · Two supplies in series and Kirchhoff’s voltage law

Two channels of the power supply are stacked: 1 V on top of 2 V, both “+ up”, driving a 300 Ω resistor. KVL says that if you walk once around a loop, adding voltage rises and subtracting drops, you end up back at 0. Press play to walk the loop.

DCV

Why the reading flips sign

The DMM shows V(HI) − V(LO). Swap the leads and you measure the same voltage from the other end, so 3 V becomes −3 V. Neither is wrong; the sign tells you which end is higher.

Why the ammeter goes in series

Current has to flow through the meter to be counted. So you open the loop and let the meter close it. Put “I” toward the + of the 1 V supply and “LO” toward the resistor so the reading comes out positive.

Why set a 100 mA current limit

The circuit only needs 10 mA. A 100 mA limit means a wiring mistake (like the one in section 1) puts the supply into CC instead of destroying something.

3 · Measuring resistance: 2-wire, 4-wire, series and parallel

In Ω mode the DMM pushes a small known current through the part and measures the voltage, then divides: R = V/I. In 2-wire mode the same two leads carry the current and sense the voltage, so the leads’ own resistance gets added in. In 4-wire mode a second pair of “sense” leads measures the voltage right at the part while carrying almost no current, so lead resistance drops out.

4 · Powered circuits and the meter’s own 10 MΩ

Two 10 MΩ resistors in series across 5 V should split it evenly: 2.5 V each. But the voltmeter is itself a 10 MΩ resistor. When you clip it across one resistor, you put 10 MΩ in parallel with 10 MΩ (5 MΩ), and the divider changes. Toggle between an ideal meter and the real one.

Ω mode in a powered circuit (steps 24–25): the ohmmeter assumes the only voltage it sees is from its own test current. The circuit’s 2.5 V adds to or subtracts from that, so one lead order reads far too high and the other reads near zero or negative. Resistance readings in a powered circuit are meaningless: always turn the power off first.
